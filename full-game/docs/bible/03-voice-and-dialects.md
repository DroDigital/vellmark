# Voice and dialects

## Writing rules

- **Use only printable ASCII in all game text.** No curly quotes, dashes longer than a hyphen, accents, or special symbols.
- Write short lines. A speaking line should rarely run past two sentences. The longest speeches belong to people who enjoy hearing themselves.
- Be specific. Name the goat. State the debt. Give the date on the receipt.
- **Stakes first on the main path.** A main quest scene opens on danger, loss, or a decision, and closes on one. Paperwork is the world's texture and never the reason to care: a player should be afraid for a village before they are told its tax status. Keep to one dry joke in a main quest scene, never as its first or last line. Side quests, books, small tasks, and gravestones keep the humour at full strength. This governs new text and how beat sheets are expanded. It never changes a line that is already written out in `docs/script/`.
- Let jokes stand. Do not explain them, and do not have other characters laugh.
- Grief is plain. When something truly terrible happens, cut the adjectives.
- Nobody says "the chosen one," "destiny awaits," or "ancient evil."
- Do not use "whispers," "shadows," "echoes," "tapestry," or "dance of" as decoration.
- Avoid the pattern "It is not X. It is Y." as a default rhythm.
- NPCs have jobs and problems unrelated to the player. Most would prefer to be doing something else.
- The player's dialogue options are short and speakable. Offer warmth, irritation, and curiosity, in addition to agreement.

## Examples

**Weak:** "Beware, traveler, for a great darkness approaches from the north."

**Right:** "The north's gone white again. Thought you should know before you buy a farm."

**Weak:** "My village... it is gone. All of them... gone."

**Right:** "There was a village here. I can tell because I have a key and nothing it opens."

## Ledger voice

The Corrector, Redactor notices, and stone inscriptions use bureaucratic present tense. They are never angry.

> ENTRY 4112.11.08.3392. A crow alights. Wind from the north. Nobody is here.

> NOTICE OF CORRECTION. The following has been found to be surplus. No action is required of you. You will not recall this notice.

## Author marginalia

Each Author writes distinctively.

| Author | Style | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Rubricator | Terse verdicts in capitals | PAID. NOT PAID. PAID IN FULL BY OTHERS. |
| Mother Vellum | Warm, bodily, unsettling | Eat, little one. All of it. There will be room. |
| Compositor | Pedantic corrections | Align this. Who taught you to build a stair. |
| Gall | Grudging tenderness | I kept this one a while longer. Do not ask why. |
| Gilder | Flattering sales pitch | You, of all people, deserve this at the old price. |
| Saint Slip | Apologies and crossings-out | I meant to fix that. Sorry. Sory. Sorry. |

## Dialects

Use these lightly, with one or two features per line. Readability always wins.

### Scrivenhold Vale

Legal, fussy, and precise. People qualify every claim and cite things.

- Features: "as per," "in respect of," "without prejudice," and exact numbers.
- "Without prejudice, that is the worst pie I have eaten since the ninth of Harrow."
- Grudge: everyone else's bad paperwork.

### The Whitemarch

Few words. Sentences drop their subjects. Winter is the assumed subject of most complaints.

- Features: dropped pronouns, "aye," "she" for the weather, and understatement.
- "Lost three toes. Kept the good ones."
- Grudge: Scrivenhold taxes firewood it has never had to carry.

### The Foxing Wood

Slow, courteous, and given to very long memories. People refer to events centuries past as recent.

- Features: "my grandmother's grandmother said," "not long ago," and courtesy before insults.
- "With the greatest respect, your family has been a disappointment since the flood."
- Grudge: loggers, and one particular family of loggers from 900 Lines ago.

### Saltmargin

Cheerful fatalism. Everything is compared with the sea, and nothing is ever worth worrying about.

- Features: "love," "mind," "could be worse," and nautical verbs for everyday acts.
- "Could be worse, love. Could be high tide."
- Grudge: inland people who say "the seaside."

### The Blotmere

Soft, sly, and slow to answer, with questions in place of statements.

- Features: tag questions such as "isn't it," "would you say," and a habit of answering with a question.
- "Dry out here, is it? For a swamp, would you say?"
- Grudge: every map ever drawn of the place.

### The Cinder Folio

Loud, blunt, and warm. Insults are friendly and compliments are threats.

- Features: shouting names, trade metaphors, and "by the red pen."
- "Sit down, you ruined hinge, and eat."
- Grudge: the Rubrication Wars, which they consider ongoing.

### The Erasure

The few who remain leave gaps in speech, losing words they no longer have. Show the loss with a plain hyphen or a substituted vague noun.

- "My - my the small one. The one who runs. Do you know what I mean?"

### The Verso

Everything reversed. Spoken lines may have inverted meanings. Reading mode displays them correctly. Inhabitants say "goodbye" as a greeting and thank you for things you are about to do.

## Player character

The player has no recorded voice and no fixed backstory. Dialogue options should allow for any people, gender, or temperament. NPCs never assign a gender to the player.

## Naming

- **Vale:** solid clerical names: Edmund Tally, Maud Ellery, Hester Quillon.
- **Whitemarch:** one-syllable given names with place surnames: Bryn of the Cross, Tor Hask.
- **Foxing Wood:** tree and courtesy names: Madam Alder Rue, Sorrel Longmemory.
- **Saltmargin:** nicknames everyone has forgotten the reason for: Nan Twice, Old Dry Peter.
- **Blotmere:** soft and repeated sounds: Lulu Mere, Ollo Lull.
- **Cinder Folio:** craft surnames: Gudrun Anvil, Tace Bellows.
- Never reuse a given name for two named NPCs.
